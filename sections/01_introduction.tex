\section*{1. Introduction}
Artificial intelligence supports biological research through predictive modeling and the generation and evaluation of hypotheses \citep{Cui2024scGPT,Theodoris2023Geneformer,Roohani2024BioDiscoveryAgent}. Self-improving agents automate parts of model development \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist,Zhou2026DrugEvolve} through an iterative process of proposing, testing and revising \citep{Madaan2023SelfRefine,Shinn2023Reflexion,Huang2023MLAgentBench}. An agent proposes changes to a model design or its training hyperparameters, trains and evaluates the candidate, and uses the results to inform the next proposal.

In biology, useful data are often distributed across laboratories studying different cell types, perturbations or experimental assays. Data-sharing constraints can prevent these measurements from being pooled \citep{Sheller2020,Bakhtiari2025FedscGen,Dayan2021FederatedCOVID,Kaissis2020PrivacyPreserving,Rieke2020DigitalHealth}. Collaborative learning enables laboratories to train a shared model through locally computed updates \citep{McMahan2017,Wang2024scFed,Li2020FedProx,Karimireddy2020SCAFFOLD,Kairouz2021Advances}. Conventional protocols commonly specify the model design and training hyperparameters before fitting begins, limiting how local biological and experimental differences inform subsequent design choices.

Local development evidence provides a complementary research signal: it records how candidate configurations perform, how their training trajectories evolve and which trials succeed or fail. Sharing these summaries allows laboratories to inform subsequent experiments while retaining their measurements locally. \textit{Can an iterative research process use laboratory-local training and evaluation to guide model development without pooling the underlying data?}

BioCoLoop extends collaboration from parameter fitting to model development by sharing both model updates and laboratory-local development evidence. Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms} places this framework alongside centralized self-improving agents and conventional collaborative training. An inner loop fits and evaluates candidates across laboratories. An outer loop uses the resulting evidence to revise proposals. Collaborative participation supplies training data and evaluation panels; development-history feedback uses their measured outcomes to guide search. We also evaluate a separate policy that uses early development trajectories to allocate further training.

We evaluate BioCoLoop on drug--target interaction prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy prediction and cell perturbation identification, with task-adapted AI-Scientist-v2 and AI-Researcher baselines and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and GPT-5.6 Luna as proposal generators. BioCoLoop improves most primary endpoints. Controlled studies examine laboratory participation, source compatibility, proposal-history feedback and training allocation. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt}
\item We introduce a collaborative research framework that uses laboratory-local development evidence, alongside model updates, to guide iterative model development without centralizing raw data.
\item We develop an inner loop for collaborative training and evaluation and an outer loop for evidence-guided proposal revision, with a common execution interface and persistent development history. A separate allocation policy uses early trajectories to direct training.
\item Across drug--target interaction prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy prediction and cell perturbation identification, we report gains on most primary endpoints, earlier discovery of useful configurations in selected searches, and improved training allocation at matched budgets.
\end{itemize}
