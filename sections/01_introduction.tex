\section*{1. Introduction}
Artificial intelligence is becoming an experimental tool for biology: models predict molecular interactions, connect protein responses to drug effects, and estimate how cells respond to perturbations. The emerging vision of an AI virtual cell places these capabilities within a broader process of generating and testing biological hypotheses \citep{Bunne2024VirtualCell,Sun2026ProteinTalks}. Research agents extend this direction from prediction to model development. They propose computational changes, run experiments and use the results to revise their next proposal \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist,Zhou2026DrugEvolve}. This iterative process offers a route from manually designed predictors toward self-improving biological AI. Yet existing research loops generally assume that the data and evaluation signals needed to assess each proposal are centrally accessible.

In biology, useful measurements are often distributed across institutions and data holders. One laboratory may study a different cell type, another a different stimulus, and a third a different experimental assay. Data-sharing constraints can prevent these measurements from being pooled \citep{Sheller2020,Bakhtiari2025FedscGen}. Collaborative learning addresses this constraint by aggregating locally trained model updates, but typically optimizes a specified model design \citep{McMahan2017,Wang2024scFed}. This creates a gap between collaborative learning and automated model development: distributed measurements can update model parameters, but they rarely participate in deciding how the model itself should be developed. \textit{Can laboratory-local measurements also guide which designs to try, which training choices to retain, and what to investigate next?}

Useful evidence may be held elsewhere: one laboratory studies a different cell type, another measures responses under a different stimulus, and a third uses a different experimental assay. Data-sharing restrictions can prevent these measurements from being brought together \citep{Sheller2020,Bakhtiari2025FedscGen}. The opportunity is therefore broader than learning from additional examples. Can measurements from different laboratories also inform which model designs to try, which training choices to retain, and what to investigate next, without transferring the underlying data?

In this paper, we introduce BioCoLoop, a collaborative research framework that extends collaboration from parameter fitting to model development itself. Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms} contrasts this setting with centralized research agents and conventional collaborative training. In BioCoLoop, each laboratory trains and evaluates candidate designs on its local measurements. The coordinator aggregates model updates for parameter fitting and evaluation evidence for research decisions, while a research controller uses the accumulated evidence to propose subsequent designs. Raw measurements remain within each laboratory.

Collaborative training allows several data holders to fit a specified predictor through local computation and model-update aggregation \citep{McMahan2017,Wang2024scFed}. Our setting makes distributed measurements available for a further purpose: evaluating candidate designs and informing subsequent research decisions. Here, a laboratory denotes a data holder that performs local training and evaluation. The coordinator aggregates model updates and evaluation evidence; the research controller proposes designs.

BioCoLoop turns proposed model changes into distributed experiments and accumulates their outcomes as reusable research evidence. This evidence guides subsequent design proposals and training allocation, which we study through history-guided proposal revision and trajectory-guided training allocation. We evaluate the framework on drug--target interaction prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy prediction, and cell perturbation identification. Across these tasks, broader laboratory participation improves predictive performance, while evidence-guided decisions can improve how research effort is allocated under a fixed computational budget.

Our contributions are summarized as:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{A new research setting for distributed model development.}
We formulate a research setting in which distributed biological measurements contribute not only to parameter fitting, but also to the empirical evidence that guides model development.

\item \textit{A new framework for collaborative model development.}
We introduce BioCoLoop, a framework that couples collaboration across distributed laboratories with an iterative model-development loop. The collaborative component aggregates local model updates and evaluation evidence without centralizing raw data, while the loop accumulates this evidence to iteratively revise model designs and decide which candidates receive further training.

\item \textit{A cross-task evaluation of collaborative model development.}
We evaluate BioCoLoop on drug-target interaction prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy prediction, and cell perturbation identification. Broader laboratory participation improves predictive performance under a fixed research policy, while controlled studies show that accumulated evaluation evidence can improve research decisions under a fixed computational budget.
\end{itemize}
