\section*{1. Introduction}
Artificial intelligence is becoming an experimental tool for biology: models predict molecular interactions, connect protein responses to drug effects, and estimate how cells respond to perturbations. The emerging vision of an AI virtual cell places these capabilities within a broader process of generating and testing biological hypotheses \citep{Bunne2024VirtualCell,Sun2026ProteinTalks}. Research agents extend this direction from prediction to model development. They propose computational changes, run experiments and use the results to revise their next proposal \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist,Zhou2026DrugEvolve}. This iterative process offers a route from manually designed predictors toward self-improving biological AI.

A biological research agent, however, usually works with the data available in its own execution environment. Useful evidence may be held elsewhere: one laboratory studies a different cell type, another measures responses under a different stimulus, and a third uses a different experimental assay. Data-sharing restrictions can prevent these measurements from being brought together \citep{Sheller2020,Bakhtiari2025FedscGen}. The opportunity is therefore broader than learning from additional examples. Can measurements from different laboratories also inform which model designs to try, which training choices to retain, and what to investigate next, without transferring the underlying data?

Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms} contrasts the relevant research settings. Centralized agents use accessible experiment outcomes to revise a predictor. Collaborative training allows several data holders to fit a specified predictor through local computation and model-update aggregation \citep{McMahan2017,Wang2024scFed}. Our setting makes distributed measurements available for a further purpose: evaluating candidate designs and informing subsequent research decisions. Here, a laboratory denotes a data holder; its computational worker carries out local training and evaluation.

We introduce BioCoLoop, a collaborative research framework in which laboratory-local evidence drives both predictive learning and iterative model development. A research controller proposes a change to the prediction components or training configuration. Laboratory workers fit and evaluate the resulting candidate, and the coordinator summarizes their measurements in an \emph{evidence card}. Each card records what changed, how training progressed and whether the candidate improved on the current predictor. The history of these cards informs the next proposal. This common research interface supports different biological predictors, while their task-specific inputs and objectives remain with the local training workers.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textbf{A collaborative setting for biological model development.} BioCoLoop provides a common training and research interface through which data holders contribute local model updates and evaluation evidence to the improvement of a task-specific predictor.
\item \textbf{An evidence-guided research harness.} The loop turns a proposed hypothesis into an executable design, records its measured outcome and uses the accumulated history to guide the next proposal. A separate trajectory-guided allocation study examines how the same measurements can help decide which candidates receive further training.
\item \textbf{An empirical account of when collaboration helps.} Across drug--target interaction prediction, observed-response proteomics and cell perturbation identification, the complete system leads four of five main endpoints. Controlled studies distinguish gains from additional data, differences among source scenarios, proposal feedback and training allocation. They show that source compatibility and task-specific learning trajectories matter alongside the number of participating laboratories.
\end{itemize}
