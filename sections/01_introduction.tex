\section*{1. Introduction}
Biological data are often distributed across institutions and data holders, where privacy and data-sharing constraints limit centralized access. Complementary measurements of molecular interactions, protein responses and cellular perturbations could improve predictive models, but cannot always be pooled across laboratories \citep{Bakhtiari2025FedscGen,Sheller2020}. A key challenge for self-improving AI in this setting is to use such distributed evidence to improve both model parameters and the computational solutions built around them.

Collaborative learning typically optimizes parameters within a predefined model design through client-local updates \citep{McMahan2017,Sheller2020,Wang2024scFed,Ding2026Tabula}, whereas automated model design and research generally rely on centralized training and evaluation signals \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist,Zhou2026DrugEvolve}. Bridging the two is nontrivial: distributed evidence must support not only parameter optimization, but also the evaluation and refinement of computational changes that generalize beyond the local settings in which they were proposed. A locally improved fit need not be a better shared predictor, and a computational revision selected by one data holder may fail on measurements held elsewhere. The central question is therefore how local evidence can jointly drive parameter learning and executable proposal refinement.

To tackle this problem, we introduce AI4AI4Cell, a collaborative research framework coupling client-local parameter fitting with a distributed proposal--evaluation loop. An inner process distributes a candidate predictor to participating laboratories, fits it on local measurements and aggregates the resulting model updates. An outer process uses aggregate development evidence to formulate a hypothesis, instantiate an executable revision and decide whether to retain it for the next experiment. Local measurements thus provide feedback at both levels: they update predictive parameters and supply evidence for accepting or rejecting computational changes. The research language model remains fixed and receives configurations and aggregate diagnostics rather than raw biological responses. The resulting objects are both a shared predictor and an executable computational design that can be evaluated across participating laboratories.

We instantiate the same training orchestration, proposer, candidate library, budget and retention rule in three biological settings: drug--target interaction (DTI) prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy classification, and five-option cell perturbation identification spanning single-gene, double-gene and drug perturbations. Each study uses ten simulated laboratories. The main comparison contrasts a fixed model or direct optimizer using one laboratory with the complete collaborative research framework using ten, while a six-arm factorial study crosses participation with fixed, direct-search and loop-enabled training. Our contributions are (i) a collaborative research framework connecting client-local parameter fitting to distributed proposal evaluation; (ii) a common executable interface across three biological task families, in which local measurements can influence both model parameters and computational designs without being sent to the research model; and (iii) a controlled protocol that separates laboratory participation, design search and iterative feedback and tests whether selected changes generalize to held-out data. Together, these experiments demonstrate the framework's applicability and establish a practical foundation for self-improving biological AI under data-sharing constraints.
