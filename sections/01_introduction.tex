\section*{1. Introduction}
Biological data are often distributed across institutions and data holders, where privacy and data-sharing constraints limit centralized access. Complementary measurements of molecular interactions, protein responses and cellular perturbations could improve predictive models, but cannot always be pooled across laboratories \citep{Bakhtiari2025FedscGen,Sheller2020}. A key challenge for self-improving AI in this setting is to use such distributed evidence to improve both model parameters and the computational solutions built around them.

Collaborative learning typically optimizes parameters within a predefined model design through client-local updates \citep{McMahan2017,Sheller2020,Wang2024scFed,Ding2026Tabula}, whereas automated model design and research generally rely on centralized training and evaluation signals \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist,Zhou2026DrugEvolve}. Bridging the two is nontrivial: distributed evidence must support not only parameter optimization, but also the evaluation and refinement of computational changes that generalize beyond the local settings in which they were proposed. A locally improved fit need not be a better shared predictor, and a computational revision selected by one data holder may fail on measurements held elsewhere. The central question is therefore how local evidence can jointly drive parameter learning and executable proposal refinement.

To tackle this problem, we introduce AI4AI4Cell, a collaborative research framework coupling client-local parameter fitting with an evidence-guided research harness. Its inner training pipeline distributes a candidate predictor to participating laboratories, fits it on local measurements and aggregates the resulting model updates. Its outer loop reads a task contract and the accumulated experiment history, formulates a focused hypothesis, compiles it into an executable model or optimization revision, evaluates the revision across local development shards, and retains the stronger design. Local measurements therefore play two distinct and complementary roles: training shards update predictive parameters, while development shards produce aggregate evidence for selecting the next computational design. Each trial---including rejected and failed trials---adds a compact evidence card to the research history, allowing subsequent proposals to build on what was tried and measured without exposing raw biological responses to the research model. The resulting objects are both a shared predictor and a reproducible design trajectory.

We instantiate the same training pipeline, proposer, candidate library, budget and retention rule in three biological settings: drug--target interaction (DTI) prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy classification, and five-option cell perturbation identification spanning single-gene, double-gene and drug perturbations. Each study uses ten simulated laboratories. The main comparison contrasts a fixed model or direct optimizer using one laboratory with the complete collaborative research framework using ten, while controlled ablations separate participation, design search and iterative feedback. Our contributions are (i) a collaborative biological research framework that links client-local parameter fitting to distributed evaluation of executable designs; (ii) an evidence-guided harness that structures each proposal as a hypothesis, experiment, expected effect and machine-resolvable design, packages every outcome into persistent history, and uses early evidence to promote computational designs for full validation; and (iii) a common evaluation across three biological task families, including a compute-matched racing experiment that holds data, candidates, total client-epochs and development measurements fixed. Together, these components provide a concrete route for distributed biological evidence to improve both predictors and the computational procedures that produce them.
