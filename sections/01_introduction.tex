\section*{1. Introduction}
Biological data are often distributed across institutions and data holders, where privacy and data-sharing constraints limit centralized access \citep{Sheller2020,Bakhtiari2025FedscGen}. Combining these measurements could improve models of molecular interactions, protein responses and cellular perturbations. Exploiting new measurements, however, involves more than refitting parameters: researchers also choose prediction heads, adjust optimization settings and decide which candidate models deserve further training. Automating this repeated process of proposing, testing and revising computational designs motivates self-improving biological AI. The challenge is to support that process when each laboratory retains its own data.

Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms} contrasts two complementary approaches. A centralized research agent iterates on data accessible to its execution environment, using experiment outcomes to revise candidate solutions. AIDE and the AI Scientist automate this process for computational research \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist}; DrugEvolve applies algorithm revision, evaluation and memory to drug-development tasks \citep{Zhou2026DrugEvolve,DrugEvolveCode}. These workflows turn measurements into design feedback. Collaborative learning instead connects several data holders through local training and model-update aggregation \citep{McMahan2017,Sheller2020,Wang2024scFed}. Here, a \emph{client} is a laboratory's computational worker, which operates on data held by that laboratory. This approach broadens the evidence available for fitting a specified model. Connecting the two requires a second shared interface: laboratories must also evaluate candidate designs and supply feedback that the research agent can use to choose its next experiment.

AI4AI4Cell couples these interfaces in a two-level research cycle. For any candidate design, an inner training process fits a shared predictor using laboratory-local data. An outer research loop uses the resulting development evidence to propose and select subsequent designs. In our implementation, a design specifies the trainable prediction head and its learning rate, regularization and aggregation settings. The loop packages each hypothesis, executed configuration, learning trajectory and selection outcome into an \emph{evidence card}. Accumulated cards condition the next proposal; early trajectories can also guide which candidates receive full training. Local measurements thus contribute both parameter updates and evidence for research decisions, while the language model receives aggregate diagnostics rather than raw biological responses.

We evaluate the framework in three biological settings: drug--target interaction (DTI) prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy classification, and cell perturbation identification spanning single-gene, double-gene and drug interventions. The main comparison uses ten simulated laboratories for the collaborative system and one for its local references. Controlled comparisons then distinguish the effects of data access, design search and feedback-dependent allocation of training compute.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we connect laboratory-local parameter fitting to distributed evaluation of executable model designs through a common biological research interface. Second, we develop an evidence-guided loop mechanism that packages proposal outcomes into reusable research history and uses measured learning trajectories to select candidates for further validation. Third, we evaluate the shared mechanism across three task families, with matched-budget comparisons and sensitivity studies of laboratory participation, data sources and proposal budget. The contribution is this coupling and its experimental evaluation, with task-specific predictors connected through the same training and research interfaces.
