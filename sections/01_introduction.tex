\section*{1. Introduction}
Biological laboratories generate valuable measurements for predicting molecular interactions, protein responses and cellular perturbations. Privacy and data-sharing constraints often require these measurements to remain at their original institutions \citep{Sheller2020,Bakhtiari2025FedscGen}. How can laboratories use their collective experimental evidence to improve a predictor while retaining their own data? Beyond fitting shared parameters, we aim to automate a second part of model development: choosing and revising prediction components and training configurations using feedback from participating laboratories. This calls for a collaborative research process in which local evidence informs both model training and the next design experiment.

Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms} contrasts two complementary approaches. A centralized research agent iterates on data accessible to its execution environment, using experiment outcomes to revise candidate solutions. AIDE and the AI Scientist automate this process for computational research \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist}; DrugEvolve applies algorithm revision, evaluation and memory to drug-development tasks \citep{Zhou2026DrugEvolve,DrugEvolveCode}. These workflows turn measurements into design feedback. Collaborative learning instead connects several data holders through local training and model-update aggregation \citep{McMahan2017,Sheller2020,Wang2024scFed}. Here, a \emph{client} is a laboratory's computational worker, which operates on data held by that laboratory. This approach broadens the evidence available for fitting a specified model. Connecting the two requires a second shared interface: laboratories must also evaluate candidate designs and supply feedback that the research agent can use to choose its next experiment.

BioCoLoop couples these interfaces in a two-level research cycle. For any candidate design, an inner training process fits a shared predictor using laboratory-local data. An outer research loop uses the resulting development evidence to propose and select subsequent designs. We define an executable design by its trainable prediction components and its learning-rate, regularization and aggregation settings. These choices let the loop adapt how a predictor learns while keeping the biological task and laboratory-local training interface fixed. The loop packages each hypothesis, executed configuration, learning trajectory and selection outcome into an \emph{evidence card}. Accumulated cards condition the next proposal; early trajectories can also guide which candidates receive full training. Local measurements thus contribute both parameter updates and evidence for research decisions, while the language model receives aggregate diagnostics rather than raw biological responses.

We evaluate the framework in three biological settings: drug--target interaction (DTI) prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy classification, and cell perturbation identification spanning single-gene, double-gene and drug interventions. Each task uses its own predictor, connected to the same training and research interfaces. The main comparison uses ten simulated laboratories for the collaborative system and one for its local references.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we connect laboratory-local parameter fitting to distributed evaluation of executable model designs through a common biological research interface. Second, we develop an evidence-guided loop mechanism that packages proposal outcomes into reusable research history and uses measured learning trajectories to select candidates for further validation. Third, we evaluate BioCoLoop across the three task families above. Controlled comparisons separate the effects of laboratory participation, proposal feedback and training-budget allocation. We also examine how performance changes with the research model, the number of participating laboratories, additional independent datasets and the number of research iterations.
