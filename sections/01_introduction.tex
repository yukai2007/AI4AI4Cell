\section*{1. Introduction}
Biological data are often distributed across institutions and data holders. Complementary measurements of molecular interactions, protein responses and cellular perturbations could improve predictive models, but privacy, ownership and unpublished-work constraints limit centralized access \citep{Bakhtiari2025FedscGen,Sheller2020}. A key challenge for self-improving AI in this setting is to use such distributed evidence to improve both model parameters and the computational solutions built around them.

Collaborative parameter training and automated research address different parts of this problem. Local-update methods fit a shared predictor from client-held observations \citep{McMahan2017,Sheller2020,Wang2024scFed,Ding2026Tabula}; research agents propose and test computational changes using evaluation feedback \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist,Zhou2026DrugEvolve}. Connecting these capabilities requires an explicit interface between candidate designs, local training and development evidence. A locally better fit is not necessarily a better shared predictor, and a locally attractive computational change may fail on other measurements. The question is therefore whether distributed evidence improves parameter fitting, design selection, or both. We study this interface rather than propose a new parameter-aggregation algorithm.

AI4AI4Bio couples these requirements in one collaborative research framework. An inner process distributes a candidate predictor to participating laboratories, fits it on client-local measurements and aggregates the resulting updates. An outer proposal--evaluation loop then converts aggregate development evidence into a hypothesis, an executable revision and a subsequent experiment. Local measurements provide feedback at both levels: they change parameters through distributed fitting, and they supply scalar development scores that retain or reject the candidate design. The research language model remains fixed and never receives raw biological responses; the objects being improved are the predictor's parameters and its executable configuration. Saved hypotheses, configurations, training receipts and selection decisions make the process inspectable.

We use common training orchestration, a proposer, candidate library, budget and retention rule with task-specific adapters in three biological settings: drug--target interaction (DTI) prediction, observed-response proteomic efficacy classification, and five-option cell perturbation identification spanning single-gene, double-gene and drug perturbations. Each study uses ten simulated laboratories. The main comparison contrasts a fixed model or direct optimizer with one laboratory against the collaborative harness with ten; a six-arm ablation crosses participation with fixed, direct-search and loop-enabled training. Our contributions are (i) an executable interface connecting client-local fitting to proposal evaluation without sending raw responses to the research model; (ii) a common implementation across three biological task families; and (iii) a protocol separating laboratory participation, design search and iterative feedback, including revisions that do not improve held-out generalization. The current draft reports executed, post-selection held-out measurements and identifies unfinished evaluations. Earlier source-search studies remain separately labeled supporting evidence rather than substitutes for missing uniform-protocol results.
