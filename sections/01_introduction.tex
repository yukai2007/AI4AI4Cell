\section*{1. Introduction}
Artificial intelligence supports biological research through predictive modeling and the generation and evaluation of hypotheses \citep{Cui2024scGPT,Theodoris2023Geneformer,Roohani2024BioDiscoveryAgent}. Self-improving agents automate parts of model development \citep{Jiang2025AIDE,Lu2024AIScientist,Zhou2026DrugEvolve} through an iterative process of proposing, testing and revising \citep{Madaan2023SelfRefine,Shinn2023Reflexion,Huang2023MLAgentBench}. An agent proposes changes to a model design or its training hyperparameters, trains and evaluates the candidate, and uses the results to inform the next proposal.

In biology, useful data are often distributed across laboratories studying different cell types, perturbations or experimental assays. Data-sharing constraints can prevent these measurements from being pooled \citep{Sheller2020,Bakhtiari2025FedscGen,Dayan2021FederatedCOVID,Kaissis2020PrivacyPreserving,Rieke2020DigitalHealth}. Collaborative learning enables laboratories to train a shared model through locally computed updates \citep{McMahan2017,Wang2024scFed,Li2020FedProx,Karimireddy2020SCAFFOLD,Kairouz2021Advances}. Automated federated design already combines configuration or system changes with distributed fitting \citep{Khodak2021FedEx,Li2025Helmsman}. These systems design or tune the training procedure; the decisions of model development---which candidates to train, how to judge them and where to invest further training---still assume that one party can evaluate them. In biology, the evidence needed for these decisions is itself distributed across laboratories.

Local development evidence provides a complementary research signal: it records how candidate configurations perform, how their training trajectories evolve and which trials succeed or fail. Sharing these summaries allows laboratories to inform subsequent experiments while retaining their measurements locally. \textit{Can an iterative research process use laboratory-local training and evaluation to guide model development without pooling the underlying data?}

BioCoLoop extends collaboration from parameter fitting to model development by sharing both model updates and laboratory-local development evidence. Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms} places this framework alongside centralized self-improving agents and conventional collaborative training. An inner loop fits and evaluates candidates across laboratories. An outer loop uses the resulting evidence to revise proposals. Collaborative participation supplies training data and evaluation panels; development-history feedback uses their measured outcomes to guide search. We also evaluate a separate policy that uses early development trajectories to allocate further training.

We evaluate BioCoLoop on three biological task families with two proposal models, conventional search controls and task-adapted research-agent baselines. Following the established federated-learning protocol \citep{Sheller2020,Chang2018Distributed,Terrail2022FLamby,WarnatHerresthal2021Swarm}, the central question is what laboratories gain by developing a model together rather than alone, measured on common held-out tests; same-access controls then characterize the search and allocation components. \begin{itemize}
\item We introduce BioCoLoop, a collaborative model-development framework in which laboratories jointly configure, train and evaluate biological predictors through a common interface while retaining raw measurements locally.
\item We show that collaboration yields substantially stronger models: the same BioCoLoop workflow improves all five primary endpoints with ten laboratories relative to one, beyond what single-laboratory random, Bayesian or exhaustive search attains, and laboratories holding distinct biological studies improve target prediction.
\item We show that collaborative development evidence also guides training allocation at a matched budget, and use same-access controls to characterize how proposal history and promotion rules contribute.
\end{itemize}
