\section*{3. Method: collaborative biological model improvement}
\subsection*{3.1 Problem setting and notation}
Consider $K$ laboratories working on the same biological prediction task. Laboratory $i$ holds training data $D_i^{\mathrm{train}}$, with $n_i=|D_i^{\mathrm{train}}|$. Evaluation uses laboratory-local development panels $\{D_i^{\mathrm{dev}}\}_{i\in\mathcal P}$, where $\mathcal P$ indexes the panels specified by the evaluation protocol. A task specification $\mathcal T$ defines the prediction inputs, outputs, training loss and evaluation metric. We write the task predictor as $f_{a,w}$, where $a\in\mathcal A$ specifies its design and $w$ its trainable parameters. Given an initial design, a design library $\mathcal A$ and a research budget, BioCoLoop seeks a design and fitted predictor that perform well on the designated evaluation population. The three task families instantiate $f_{a,w}$ as task-specific predictors within this shared research procedure.

Our design library varies four choices: learning rate, weight decay, coordinator momentum and an optional residual prediction module. A \texttt{design\_id} names one configuration of these choices. Thus, in the reported study a candidate is an executable model specification together with its training hyperparameters, not an arbitrary isolated neural block: MLP or MHA alternatives would be candidates only if they were explicitly registered in the design library. Here the only architecture toggle in the menu is a zero-initialized, width-32 residual prediction module; the task-specific backbone and prediction head are supplied by the adapter.

Here \emph{coordinator} means the trusted execution/orchestration service, not the self-improving model, a trainable neural layer, or an additional laboratory. It resolves a registered design identifier, initializes and broadcasts the predictor, coordinates local fitting, aggregates local model states, aggregates bounded development measurements, and applies the checkpoint/design-selection rule. \emph{Shared coordinator} means that all participating laboratories use this same logical protocol and decision point: they receive the same candidate state and schedule, while their raw training and development measurements remain local. The coordinator returns only the parameter state or update information needed for collaborative fitting and the declared aggregate scores/diagnostics needed for research decisions. The harness therefore coordinates candidate selection, local fitting, evaluation and the return of evidence to the research model $S$ (Figure~\ref{fig:research-interface}), a language model whose weights $\theta$ stay fixed throughout a run. We use the common fitting interface to study two policies: history-guided proposal revision (Section 3.3) and trajectory-guided training allocation (Section 3.4). The experiments simulate separate laboratories on one host.

\subsection*{3.2 Collaborative fitting and shared evaluation}
For each candidate $a$, the coordinator resets the predictor to its prescribed initialization $w_0$ and distributes it to all training laboratories. Let $\operatorname{LocalEpoch}_a$ denote one full local AdamW epoch with the candidate's learning rate and weight decay, batch size 64 and gradient-norm clipping at one. The optimizer state resets at each round. With sample weights $\pi_i=n_i/\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_j$, coordinator momentum coefficient $\beta_a$ and initial velocity $v_0=0$, round $r$ updates the trainable parameters by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_{i,r}&=\operatorname{LocalEpoch}_a(w_{r-1};D_i^{\mathrm{train}}),
&\bar w_r&=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_i u_{i,r},\\
v_r&=\beta_a v_{r-1}+\bar w_r-w_{r-1},
&w_r&=w_{r-1}+v_r.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:local-fitting}
\end{equation}
The coordinator carries momentum across aggregation rounds through $v_r$; $\beta_a=0$ gives sample-weighted parameter averaging \citep{McMahan2017}. In one round, the coordinator sends the current trainable state and fixed candidate settings to each laboratory; laboratory $i$ returns its locally fitted state $u_{i,r}$ (and its training-example count $n_i$) for aggregation. Frozen feature extractors remain unchanged. Task adapters supply the biological inputs, trainable prediction components and local loss. The laboratories never send raw examples, expression profiles, labels or per-example identifiers to the coordinator or self-improving agent.

Every five rounds and at the final round, the laboratories evaluate the aggregated predictor $f_{a,w_r}$ on the development panels indexed by $\mathcal P$. Each laboratory returns scalar primary score $s_i(a,r)$, development loss $\ell_i^{\mathrm{dev}}(a,r)$ and the bounded trajectory diagnostics required by the protocol; it does not return its underlying observations. In the main comparison these are the participating laboratories' panels; fixed-pool repartitioning retains all ten original panels, and source-transfer studies use the original target panels. Define $s_i(a,r)$ and $\ell_i^{\mathrm{dev}}(a,r)$ as the primary score and loss on panel $i$. The coordinator computes
\begin{equation}
\bar s(a,r)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}s_i(a,r),
\qquad
\bar\ell(a,r)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}\ell_i^{\mathrm{dev}}(a,r).
\label{eq:shared-evaluation}
\end{equation}
The selected round $\widehat r_a$ maximizes $\bar s(a,r)$, with lower $\bar\ell(a,r)$ breaking ties and the earlier round retained on a complete tie. Its parameters are $\widehat w_a=w_{\widehat r_a}$; the pair $q(a)=(\bar s(a,\widehat r_a),-\bar\ell(a,\widehat r_a))$ ranks the design. In plain terms, selection takes the highest equal-laboratory mean development score, then the lower mean development loss, then the earlier checkpoint. Training therefore weights examples, whereas selection weights the designated development panels equally. The main proposal-search experiments assign each candidate 100 training rounds; training duration is specified by the evaluation schedule, not by \texttt{design\_id}.
Each candidate therefore produces one \emph{development-evidence card}, and after $t$ trials the accumulated \emph{development evidence} is the ordered set
$\mathcal E_t=\{e_1,\dots,e_t\}$. Card $e_j$ records the executed configuration $a_j$, i.e. its model specification and training hyperparameters; the hypothesis $h_j$; the best development primary score and loss $(s_j,\ell_j)$ with the selected round $\widehat r_j$; the endpoint diagnostics $\Delta_j$, namely the first and last development-evaluation rounds with their sample-weighted training losses, the equal-laboratory development scores and losses at those rounds and the changes between them; the optional worst-laboratory score and evaluating-laboratory count; and the acceptance decision $\mathrm{dec}_j$. The card is therefore more than a single metric, but it is not raw data or free-form biological evidence.

For auxiliary-source studies with different prediction targets, shared encoders follow the same update rule, while each source-specific head is aggregated only over laboratories using that prediction target. Checkpoint selection then uses the original target's development panels. This allows auxiliary measurements to improve the shared representation while preserving each source's prediction objective.

\subsection*{3.3 Proposal revision from development evidence}
The first proposal is generated once without development feedback and shared by direct and loop search. History feedback begins with the second proposal. For subsequent research steps $t$, let $a_t$ be the retained design, $\mathcal E_t$ the development evidence accumulated so far and $\mathcal U_t$ the untried configurations. The research model $S$, with weights $\theta$ held fixed, generates a proposal $p_t$ and the coordinator maps its \texttt{design\_id}, denoted $\operatorname{id}(p_t)$, to the candidate:
\begin{equation}
p_t\sim P_\theta(\,\cdot\mid\mathcal T,a_t,\mathcal E_t,\mathcal U_t),
\qquad
\widetilde a_t=\operatorname{Compile}(\operatorname{id}(p_t)).
\label{eq:proposal}
\end{equation}
The proposal also contains a \texttt{hypothesis}, an \texttt{experiment} and an \texttt{expected\_effect}: these describe the motivation, intended comparison and anticipated outcome. They provide an interpretable account of the proposed experiment. The identifier determines the executed configuration, and measured development performance determines selection; the prose cannot override the registered configuration or the measured result.

After training $\widetilde a_t$, the coordinator appends the card $e_t$ of Section 3.2 to the development evidence. Using lexicographic comparison of $q$---higher score, then lower loss---the research state becomes
\begin{equation}
a_{t+1}=\begin{cases}
\widetilde a_t,&q(\widetilde a_t)>_{\mathrm{lex}}q(a_t),\\
a_t,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad \mathcal E_{t+1}=\mathcal E_t\mathbin{\|}[e_t],
\label{eq:research-update}
\end{equation}
where $\|$ appends a card to the evidence set. Unsuccessful trials remain in that set; invalid proposals and numerically failed fits consume a proposal slot and leave the incumbent unchanged. Subsequent proposals therefore respond to measured outcomes through updated context, without fine-tuning the self-improving model. After the proposal budget is exhausted, the retained design and its selected checkpoint are evaluated on the held-out task.

\subsection*{3.4 Evidence-guided training allocation}
We use trajectory-guided allocation in a controlled study (Section 4.4) to test whether early evaluation evidence improves the use of a fixed training budget. The screening--promotion policy retains both high-scoring and still-improving candidates. It screens ten predetermined designs for 20 rounds, evaluating every five rounds through Equation~\ref{eq:shared-evaluation}. Within each of three learning-rate groups, it promotes the best-scoring design and, among the remaining designs, the one with the largest score improvement from round 5 to round 20. Development loss breaks score ties. The six promoted designs restart from their initial parameters for 100-round training; their best development checkpoints determine the final predictor.

The control trains all ten designs for 80 rounds. Both schedules use 800 aggregation rounds and 160 aggregate development evaluations; the scheduler does not generate language-model proposals. This separate allocation study improves Norman Top-1 by 10.84 percentage points on average (Section 4.4). The main comparison and proposal-history experiments use 100 training rounds per candidate to compare design selection at a fixed training duration; they do not use the allocation policy. Appendix A collects the complete proposal schema, algorithm and allocation rules.
