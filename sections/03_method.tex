\section*{3. Method: collaborative agentic biological research}
\subsection*{3.1 Framework overview}
BioCoLoop takes a task specification, an initial predictor, an editable design space and data held by $K$ participating laboratories. The task specification defines prediction inputs and outputs, scorer and computational budget. Each laboratory maintains training and development partitions. A research controller proposes a design, a shared training service fits its parameters across laboratories, and local evaluators return development measurements to a coordinator. The outputs are the selected design with fitted parameters and an ordered history of proposals and outcomes (Figure~\ref{fig:research-interface}).

The key mechanism is the return of experimental evidence to the research process. An \emph{evidence card} links a proposed change to its executed configuration, aggregate learning-trajectory summary and selection outcome. Successive cards form a persistent history, allowing the controller to revise its next hypothesis using both successful and unsuccessful trials. The outer loop decides which design to investigate next; the inner process fits parameters for that design.

\subsection*{3.2 Evidence-guided proposal and revision}
For \emph{proposal revision}, the controller receives the task specification, current design and accumulated evidence cards. It returns four fields: a \texttt{hypothesis} stating why a change should help, an \texttt{experiment} describing the comparison, an \texttt{expected\_effect} identifying the predicted metric or diagnostic response, and a \texttt{design\_id} selecting prediction components and optimization settings. A validator maps the identifier to an executable configuration. The evaluation schedule specifies training duration separately from \texttt{design\_id}: the main proposal-search protocol assigns each candidate 100 training rounds. Section 3.3 defines a round; Appendix A gives the proposal schema and design library.

After fitting a candidate, the coordinator records its configuration, hypothesis, best-checkpoint statistics, first/last training and development diagnostics, and acceptance decision in an evidence card. It accepts a candidate when its selected checkpoint improves the incumbent's equal-laboratory mean development score, using lower mean development loss to break score ties. The accumulated cards, including rejected directions, enter the next proposal's context. The controller can therefore respond to new experimental outcomes while its language-model weights stay fixed.

In a separate \emph{training allocation} study, a prespecified scheduler uses the same evidence interface to screen a fixed slate of ten designs for 20 rounds each. Within each of three learning-rate strata, it selects the design with the best screening score and, from the remaining designs, the one with the largest development-score change from round 5 to round 20. These six designs restart from their initial parameters for 100-round training and development evaluation; the other four finish after screening. Uniform allocation instead trains all ten designs for 80 rounds. Both schedules spend 800 rounds over all ten laboratories. This comparison holds the candidate set fixed to isolate training allocation from proposal generation. Appendix A specifies promotion tie-breaks.

\subsection*{3.3 Local fitting and distributed evaluation}
Each candidate fit starts afresh using the task's initialization procedure and the same seed. Within a fit, all participating laboratories start with identical parameters. One training round consists of one complete local AdamW epoch per laboratory, followed by aggregation. Workers receive the current shared parameters, fit them on their local training partitions and return their fitted model states and training-example counts. The coordinator averages parameters in proportion to these counts, following FedAvg \citep{McMahan2017}, and applies server momentum when specified by the design. It distributes the updated predictor for the next round. Task adapters supply the input representation, trainable prediction components and loss to this common training pipeline.

Every five rounds and at the final round, laboratories evaluate the same aggregated checkpoint on their local development partitions. Let $s_i(a,r)$ denote laboratory $i$'s primary development score for design $a$ at round $r$. Checkpoint selection maximizes the equal-laboratory mean $S(a,r)=K^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_i(a,r)$, with lower equal-laboratory mean loss breaking score ties. The selected checkpoint represents its design in the comparison with the incumbent; complete ties retain the earlier checkpoint or incumbent. After research concludes, the selected design and checkpoint are fixed for held-out scoring.

Workers return model states and counts for training, and scalar scores and diagnostics for evaluation. The coordinator supplies aggregate evidence cards to the research language model; raw interaction rows, proteomic profiles and expression responses remain at the worker interface. We simulate these roles on one host, using $K=10$ in the main collaborative comparison and varying $K$ in the sensitivity study.
