\section*{3. Method: collaborative biological model improvement}
\subsection*{3.1 Problem setting and notation}
Consider $K$ laboratories working on the same biological prediction task. Laboratory $i$ holds training data $D_i^{\mathrm{train}}$, with $n_i=|D_i^{\mathrm{train}}|$. Evaluation uses laboratory-local development panels $\{D_i^{\mathrm{dev}}\}_{i\in\mathcal P}$, where $\mathcal P$ indexes the panels specified by the evaluation protocol. A task specification $\mathcal T$ defines the prediction inputs, outputs, training loss and evaluation metric. We write the task predictor as $f_{a,w}$, where $a\in\mathcal A$ specifies its design and $w$ its trainable parameters. Given an initial design, a design space $\mathcal A$ and a research budget, BioCoLoop seeks a design and fitted predictor that perform well on the designated evaluation population. The three task families instantiate $f_{a,w}$ as task-specific predictors within this shared research procedure.

In the original end-to-end experiments, the \emph{registered executable design space} $\mathcal A$ contains complete candidate configurations with four editable choices: learning rate, weight decay, coordinator momentum and an optional width-32 residual prediction module with zero-initialized output. Each \texttt{design\_id} selects one configuration; the task adapter supplies the backbone and prediction head. The follow-up studies expand the head, objective and scheduling choices under separately fixed protocols (Appendices E.6 and E.9).

A \emph{shared coordinator} is the trusted execution service used by all participating laboratories. It resolves each design identifier, initializes and broadcasts the predictor, coordinates local fitting, aggregates model states and bounded development measurements, and applies the checkpoint and design-selection rules. Laboratories receive the same candidate state and schedule while retaining raw training and development measurements locally. The coordinator supplies the aggregate evidence to research model $S$ (Figure~\ref{fig:research-interface}), a language model whose weights $\theta$ remain fixed throughout a run. We use this fitting interface to evaluate history-guided proposal revision (Section 3.3) and trajectory-guided training allocation (Section 3.4) separately.

\subsection*{3.2 Collaborative fitting and shared evaluation}
For each candidate $a$, the coordinator resets the predictor to its prescribed initialization $w_0$ and distributes it to all training laboratories. In the original protocol, $\operatorname{LocalEpoch}_a$ is one full local AdamW epoch with the candidate's learning rate and weight decay, batch size 64 and gradient-norm clipping at one. The optimizer state resets at each round. With sample weights $\pi_i=n_i/\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_j$, coordinator momentum coefficient $\beta_a$ and initial velocity $v_0=0$, round $r$ updates the trainable parameters by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_{i,r}&=\operatorname{LocalEpoch}_a(w_{r-1};D_i^{\mathrm{train}}),
&\bar w_r&=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_i u_{i,r},\\
v_r&=\beta_a v_{r-1}+\bar w_r-w_{r-1},
&w_r&=w_{r-1}+v_r.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:local-fitting}
\end{equation}
The coordinator carries momentum across aggregation rounds through $v_r$. With $\beta_a=0$, aggregation reduces to sample-weighted parameter averaging \citep{McMahan2017}. In each round, the coordinator sends the current trainable state and fixed candidate settings to every laboratory. Laboratory $i$ returns its locally fitted state $u_{i,r}$ and training-example count $n_i$. Frozen feature extractors remain unchanged. Task adapters supply the biological inputs, trainable prediction components and local loss; raw examples, expression profiles, labels and per-example identifiers remain local.

Every five rounds and at the final round, laboratories evaluate the aggregated predictor $f_{a,w_r}$ on the development panels indexed by $\mathcal P$. Each returns a scalar primary score $s_i(a,r)$, development loss $\ell_i^{\mathrm{dev}}(a,r)$ and the bounded trajectory diagnostics required by the protocol. The main comparison uses participating laboratories' panels; fixed-pool repartitioning retains all ten original panels; source-transfer studies use the original target panels. The coordinator computes
\begin{equation}
\bar s(a,r)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}s_i(a,r),
\qquad
\bar\ell(a,r)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal P}\ell_i^{\mathrm{dev}}(a,r).
\label{eq:shared-evaluation}
\end{equation}
The selected round $\widehat r_a$ maximizes $\bar s(a,r)$, with lower $\bar\ell(a,r)$ breaking ties and the earlier round retained on a complete tie. Its parameters are $\widehat w_a=w_{\widehat r_a}$. The pair $q(a)=(\bar s(a,\widehat r_a),-\bar\ell(a,\widehat r_a))$ ranks the design. We call $\bar s(a,\widehat r_a)$ the \emph{dev score}, labelled ``highest dev score'' in Figure~\ref{fig:research-interface}. Training weights examples, while original-protocol selection weights the designated panels equally. The original searches use 100 rounds per candidate; Appendices E.6 and E.9 specify the follow-up batch sizes, 20-round schedules and development scorers, including pooled AUROC/AP in E.9.
Each trial produces a \emph{development-evidence card}. After $t$ trials, the sequence $\mathcal E_t=(e_1,\dots,e_t)$ forms the accumulated \emph{development evidence}. For $1\le j\le t$, card $e_j$ records the executed configuration $a_j$, hypothesis $h_j$, best development primary score and loss $(s_j,\ell_j)$, selected round $\widehat r_j$ and acceptance decision $\mathrm{dec}_j$. Endpoint diagnostics $\Delta_j$ contain the first and last development-evaluation rounds, their sample-weighted training losses, equal-laboratory development scores and losses, and the changes between those measurements, plus the optional worst-laboratory score and evaluating-laboratory count.

For auxiliary-source studies with different prediction targets, shared encoders follow the same update rule, while each source-specific head is aggregated only over laboratories using that prediction target. Checkpoint selection uses the original target's development panels. Each source retains its own prediction objective while contributing updates to the shared representation.

\subsection*{3.3 Proposal revision from development evidence}
The first proposal is generated once without development feedback and shared by direct and loop search. History feedback begins with the second proposal. For subsequent research steps $t$, let $a_t$ be the retained design, $\mathcal E_t$ the development evidence accumulated so far and $\mathcal U_t$ the untried configurations. The research model $S$, with weights $\theta$ held fixed, generates a proposal $p_t$ and the coordinator maps its \texttt{design\_id}, denoted $\operatorname{id}(p_t)$, to the candidate:
\begin{equation}
p_t\sim P_\theta(\,\cdot\mid\mathcal T,a_t,\mathcal E_t,\mathcal U_t),
\qquad
\widetilde a_t=\operatorname{Compile}(\operatorname{id}(p_t)).
\label{eq:proposal}
\end{equation}
The proposal also contains a \texttt{hypothesis}, an \texttt{experiment} and an \texttt{expected\_effect}, recording the motivation, intended comparison and anticipated outcome. The identifier determines execution; measured development performance determines selection.

After training $\widetilde a_t$, the coordinator constructs the next card $e_{t+1}$. Comparing $q$ lexicographically by higher score and then lower loss gives
\begin{equation}
a_{t+1}=\begin{cases}
\widetilde a_t,&q(\widetilde a_t)>_{\mathrm{lex}}q(a_t),\\
a_t,&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad \mathcal E_{t+1}=\mathcal E_t\mathbin{\|}[e_{t+1}],
\label{eq:research-update}
\end{equation}
where $\|$ appends a card to the history. Unsuccessful trials remain in the history. Invalid proposals and numerically failed fits consume a proposal slot and leave the incumbent unchanged. Updated context carries these outcomes into subsequent proposals. After the proposal budget is exhausted, the retained design and its selected checkpoint are evaluated on the held-out task.

\subsection*{3.4 Evidence-guided training allocation}
We use trajectory-guided allocation in a controlled study (Section 4.4) to test whether early evaluation evidence improves the use of a fixed training budget. The screening--promotion policy retains both high-scoring and still-improving candidates. It screens ten predetermined designs for 20 rounds, evaluating every five rounds through Equation~\ref{eq:shared-evaluation}. Within each of three learning-rate groups, it promotes the best-scoring design and, among the remaining designs, the one with the largest score improvement from round 5 to round 20. Development loss breaks score ties. The six promoted designs restart from their initial parameters for 100-round training, and their best development checkpoints determine the final predictor.

The two promotion criteria reserve training capacity for candidates at different stages of convergence. Current score favors a strong screening checkpoint, while improvement over the shared screening interval retains a candidate whose score is still rising. Grouping by learning rate preserves representation from each group in the full-training stage. All promotion decisions use development measurements.

The control trains all ten designs for 80 rounds. Both schedules use 800 aggregation rounds and 160 aggregate development evaluations. This study evaluates allocation over a predetermined candidate set independently of proposal generation. The main comparison and proposal-history experiments use 100 training rounds per candidate. Section 4.4 reports the allocation results; Appendix A provides the complete proposal schema, algorithm and allocation rules.
