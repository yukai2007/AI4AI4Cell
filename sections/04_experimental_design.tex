\section*{4. Experiments}
\subsection*{4.1 Tasks, datasets and evaluation}
We evaluate BioCoLoop on three biological prediction problems with task-specific base models and a common procedure for collaborative training, evaluation and proposal generation. The main setting partitions each dataset into ten disjoint groups. Complementary studies use distinct biological datasets or experimental scenarios as laboratories to evaluate source transfer.

\textbf{Drug--target interactions.}
Given a compound and a protein sequence, the model predicts whether they interact. BindingDB provides 50,149 training pairs and 5,604 development pairs, with canonical compounds defining laboratory assignments. We use TAPB as the base model, with frozen ESM2 protein features and trainable drug, fusion and classification modules \citep{lin2025tapb}. The primary metric is AUROC on 5,505 random test pairs. Additional test sets containing 3,791 unseen-drug pairs and 2,507 unseen-protein pairs measure generalization to unseen entities. AP, MCC, accuracy and cross-entropy are reported as secondary metrics.

\textbf{Observed-response proteomics.}
Given a drug representation and its measured 6h and 24h proteomic responses, the model predicts binary efficacy. A ProteinTalks PTPC subset provides 386 training, 105 development and 148 test conditions \citep{Sun2026ProteinTalks}. Compound-group assignment gives each laboratory 49-50 training and development conditions. The base model is a ProteinTalks-derived efficacy model with protein and drug branches, trained from random initialization. Average precision (AP) is the primary metric. AUROC, accuracy and cross-entropy are secondary metrics. Unlike de novo response prediction, this task conditions on observed post-treatment proteomic measurements.

\textbf{Cell perturbation identification.}
Given an expression response and five candidate interventions, the model identifies the intervention whose predicted response has the highest cosine similarity to the observation. We use VCC single-gene perturbations \citep{arc2025vcc}, Norman double-gene perturbations \citep{norman2019genetic} and a Tahoe subset of drug perturbations in A549 at 5 $\mu$M \citep{zhang2025tahoe}. Intervention-disjoint training, development and test counts are 60/20/20, 70/20/27 and 180/60/67, respectively. The base model uses a frozen scDEBART backbone with a trainable response head \citep{sung2026scdebart}. All methods use the same corrected attention masks and the same candidate sets. The primary metric is intervention-macro Top-1, which first averages accuracy within each intervention and then across interventions. Random selection has an expected Top-1 of 20\%. Micro Top-1, MRR, Top-3 and cross-entropy are reported as secondary metrics. Appendix A provides the response construction, chemical conditioning, training losses, and secondary evaluation details.

\subsection*{4.2 Compared methods and shared protocol}
We compare BioCoLoop with three types of baselines. The \emph{fixed model} fits the task-specific base model once with a predefined configuration. \emph{Direct optimization} uses BioCoLoop's proposal generator and registered executable design space, generating proposals without previous development outcomes. AI-Scientist-v2 \citep{Yamada2025AIScientistV2} and AI-Researcher \citep{Tang2025AIResearcher} are adapted to the same biological tasks and fitting interface. Every method returns one predictor; laboratories contribute to its fitting, not an inference-time ensemble. \emph{Direct} means feedback-free proposal generation, not a single training run; the fixed model is the single-fit reference.

BioCoLoop combines collaborative fitting and evaluation with development-history feedback. We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and GPT-5.6 Luna as proposal generators \citep{OpenAI2026GPT56Luna}. \textbf{Evaluation protocol.} We simulate data held by separate laboratories by partitioning each training pool into disjoint laboratory shards, as in synthetic federated partitions \citep{McMahan2017,He2020FedML}; benchmarks such as LEAF instead use natural client partitions \citep{Caldas2018LEAF}, and our source-defined study (Section~4.5) follows that alternative. As in federated studies in medicine and biology \citep{Sheller2020,Chang2018Distributed,Dayan2021FederatedCOVID,Terrail2022FLamby,WarnatHerresthal2021Swarm,Wang2024scFed}, the contribution of collaboration is measured by comparing ten collaborating laboratories with laboratory 0 developing models alone; all methods are scored on the same task-specific held-out test, which never enters training, selection or proposal feedback. Section 4.7 and Appendix E additionally fix laboratories, panels, design choices and candidate budgets to characterize search at matched access.

The main comparison uses twelve configurations and at most six proposals beyond the initial design. Each valid candidate starts from the prescribed task initialization and receives 100 training rounds. Development score and loss determine the retained design and checkpoint before held-out scoring. We report means and sample standard deviations over seeds 42--44 on fixed data partitions. These summaries describe training and search variability; paired cluster-bootstrap intervals in the Statistical Supplement quantify held-out sample uncertainty. The allocation, source-transfer and extended-search studies use their separately specified budgets and seeds.
