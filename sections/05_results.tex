\subsection*{4.3 Collaborative versus single-laboratory development}
Collaboration produces substantially stronger models. Running the same history-enabled Qwen workflow with laboratory 0 alone and with ten laboratories, collaboration improves every primary endpoint on the common held-out tests: DTI AUROC 89.89$\rightarrow$93.76, proteomic AP 20.13$\rightarrow$36.99, VCC Top-1 20.22$\rightarrow$31.54, Norman 14.15$\rightarrow$22.25 and Tahoe 15.42$\rightarrow$22.39 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:single-lab-search}). The gain is not recovered by searching harder alone: single-laboratory random, Bayesian and exhaustive twelve-design search remain below the collaborative result on all five endpoints, and collaboration also improves DTI on unseen drugs and targets (Appendix D). \subsection*{4.4 Evidence-guided training allocation}
Evidence-guided allocation improves held-out performance at a matched budget (Table~\ref{tab:strong-effects}). Uniform allocation trains all ten designs for 80 rounds. The evidence-guided policy screens them for 20 rounds and restarts six selected designs for 100-round training. Each schedule uses 800 aggregation rounds and 160 development evaluations.

Norman Top-1 increases from 11.48\% to 22.32\%, a 10.84-point gain with a hierarchical paired-bootstrap 95\% interval of [4.54, 17.73]. A selected low-learning-rate model improves beyond the uniform stopping point. Across the twelve task--seed pairs, allocation yields four wins, eight ties and no losses: three gains on Norman and one on Tahoe. Screening and restarting costs are included. This fixed-slate experiment isolates training allocation from language-model proposal history.

A fresh 360-fit study adds score-only and random promotion (Table~\ref{tab:rebuttal-allocation}). On Tahoe, trajectory promotion reaches 22.89 versus 20.90 for score-only and 21.95 for random. On Norman, score-only reaches 21.70 and random 22.63, versus our 22.32; random also leads on VCC (22.49 versus 20.01). Selective longer fitting therefore explains much of the gain over uniform allocation, while the choice of promotion rule has task-dependent effects.

\subsection*{4.5 Learning from different data sources}
Collaboration remains beneficial when laboratories hold genuinely different biological studies rather than shards of one pool. We combine BindingDB with BioSNAP, Davis and Human; VCC with three external single-cell studies; and PTPC with Lin, Ruprecht and decryptE \citep{Lin2026HDAC,Ruprecht2020Proteome,Eckert2024DecryptE}. Target development data select each model, and all configurations use the same target test set.

With one proteomic study per laboratory, the three auxiliary sources raise target AP from 34.41 to 37.53 and AUROC from 72.33 to 74.39. Shared encoders combine local updates while source-specific heads retain their own objectives. Davis and Human also improve target DTI AUROC. Benefits follow source compatibility: at four laboratories and 60 training interventions in both cell arms, same-source VCC reaches 30.70\% Top-1 versus 28.82\% with external studies. Appendix B reports every source comparison; Appendix E.4 tests proposal history at matched source access.

\subsection*{4.6 Effect of the number of laboratories}
We vary $K\in\{1,2,5,10\}$ by adding laboratories and data or repartitioning a fixed pool (Figure~\ref{fig:lab-sensitivity}). Adding participating laboratories, and with them data, improves performance, which is the operating regime collaboration targets. When a fixed pool is merely repartitioned, effects depend on the task: proteomics peaks at an intermediate $K$ and Norman degrades as its pool is split further.

Both settings use the default design and training schedule. Increasing participation adds local development panels; fixed-pool repartitioning keeps all ten original evaluation panels. In cell tasks, training groups also define contrastive-negative pools. Appendix B.1 reports these optimization and evaluation differences alongside the complete results.
