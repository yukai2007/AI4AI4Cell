\subsection*{5.2 Does feedback improve the allocation of training compute?}
This experiment asks whether early development measurements help allocate a fixed training budget. The candidate designs and laboratory data are identical. Uniform allocation trains every design equally; the evidence-guided loop screens all designs and promotes a subset using current performance and learning progress. Thus Table~\ref{tab:strong-effects} measures training allocation, independently of an LLM's ability to generate better designs.

\input{tables/strong_v3/effects}

Across 12 newly executed proteomics/cell task--seed comparisons, the loop wins four and ties eight. Norman improves from 11.48\% to 22.32\% macro Top-1, a +10.84-point mean effect with hierarchical paired-bootstrap 95\% CI [4.54, 17.73]. Tahoe improves by +1.99 points, with one win and two ties; VCC and proteomics tie. On Norman, a promoted low-learning-rate residual design continues improving beyond the uniform method's stopping point. Concentrating full validation on promising candidates therefore improves the final predictor at the same total training budget. The DTI development replay is reported separately in Appendix A because it reuses existing trajectories rather than newly scored held-out runs.

The factorial comparison addresses a different question: what changes when more laboratories participate? Holding the research loop fixed, ten versus one laboratory adds \StrongDTIFederationEffect{} AUROC points for DTI, \StrongPTPCFederationEffect{} AP points for proteomics, and \StrongVCCFederationEffect{}, \StrongNormanFederationEffect{} and \StrongTahoeFederationEffect{} Top-1 points for the three cell settings. Appendix A reports all six combinations, including direct-versus-loop contrasts at fixed access. At six proposals, the original direct and loop configurations select the same checkpoints, so the racing result concerns evidence-dependent allocation rather than an independently demonstrated final-score gain from proposal memory.

\subsection*{5.3 What changes with more laboratories, sources and proposals?}
The laboratory-count study separates data availability from partitioning. With the complete pool retained, PTPC AP ranges from 34.59 at $K=1$ to 39.19 at $K=5$ and 37.54 at $K=10$. Norman instead scores 49.63 at $K=1$ and 15.48 at $K=10$ under the default recipe. Merging clients changes both local optimization and, for cell tasks, the contrastive negative pool. These results make laboratory count a meaningful design parameter rather than a setting whose benefit can be assumed to increase monotonically.

External sources have different effects on the same target. Adding Jiang data raises VCC Top-1 from 29.49 to 33.07; adding all three external studies gives 27.33. The Jiang difference is +3.58 points with a paired intervention-bootstrap 95\% CI of [$-$2.11, 9.51]. For proteomics, combining all three mtPTDS contexts changes AP from 34.59 to 35.47. Source compatibility, rather than source count alone, therefore deserves explicit evaluation. Appendix B retains every source combination and its uncertainty.

Longer search also distinguishes development efficiency from held-out improvement. In the completed Norman seed-61 trace, Luna reaches its best development primary score at proposal slot 4 with feedback and slot 22 without feedback, but both finish at 23.11\% test Top-1 after 24 slots. Qwen likewise finishes tied at 22.30\%. On VCC seed 61, both Qwen modes fall from 31.50\% test Top-1 at six slots to 19.61\% at 24 despite improved development performance. The ongoing multi-seed study reports proposal validity and prefix curves alongside scores, making it possible to distinguish execution reliability, faster search and improved generalization.
