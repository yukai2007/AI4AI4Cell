\subsection*{4.4 Evidence-guided training allocation}
Evidence-guided allocation improves held-out performance at a matched budget (Table~\ref{tab:strong-effects}). Uniform allocation trains all ten designs for 80 rounds. The evidence-guided policy screens them for 20 rounds and restarts six selected designs for 100-round training. Each schedule uses 800 aggregation rounds and 160 development evaluations.
\input{tables/strong_v3/effects}

Across twelve task--seed comparisons, allocation yields four wins, eight ties and no losses. Norman Top-1 increases from 11.48\% to 22.32\%, a 10.84-point gain with a hierarchical paired-bootstrap 95\% interval of [4.54, 17.73]. A selected low-learning-rate model improves beyond the uniform stopping point. Tahoe improves in one seed and ties in two; proteomics and VCC tie in all three. Screening and restarting costs are included. This is an allocation result over a fixed candidate set, independent of language-model proposal history.

A fresh 360-fit comparison reproduces these means and adds simple promotion controls (Appendix E.5). On Norman, score-only promotion reaches 21.70 and the exact random-promotion expectation is 22.63, versus 22.32 for score-plus-trajectory promotion. On Tahoe, the trajectory policy reaches 22.89 versus 20.90 and 21.95, respectively. Random promotion also outperforms the trajectory policy on VCC (22.49 versus 20.01). Thus selective longer fitting improves on uniform allocation, but the trajectory criterion does not consistently outperform simpler promotion rules.

\subsection*{4.5 Learning from different data sources}
Source-transfer studies use laboratories from distinct biological datasets or experimental settings. We combine BindingDB with BioSNAP, Davis and Human; VCC with three external single-cell studies; and PTPC with Lin, Ruprecht and decryptE \citep{Lin2026HDAC,Ruprecht2020Proteome,Eckert2024DecryptE}. Target development data select each model, and all configurations use the same target test set.

Transfer gains depend on the source. Adding all three proteomic studies improves target AP, and Davis and Human improve DTI AUROC. With laboratory and training-condition counts matched, external single-cell studies yield lower target Top-1 than additional data from VCC. Appendix B reports the source-specific effects and matched controls.

In the one-study-per-laboratory proteomic protocol, adding Lin, Ruprecht and decryptE raises target AP from 34.41 to 37.53 and AUROC from 72.33 to 74.39. Shared encoders receive updates from all participating sources, while each source-specific head retains its own prediction objective. The original target development panels select checkpoints throughout this comparison. The matched cell study uses four laboratories and 60 training interventions in each arm: the same-source VCC split reaches 30.70\% Top-1, compared with 28.82\% for VCC plus external studies. Both arms share the same target anchor; measured gene panels and underlying cell counts remain source-specific.

These source-defined laboratories retain differences between released studies, although all workers run on one host. Appendix B reports source-specific transfer; Appendix E adds three-seed same-source-access direct/history controls, which do not show a general history advantage.

\subsection*{4.6 Effect of the number of laboratories}
We vary $K\in\{1,2,5,10\}$ by either adding laboratories and training data or repartitioning a fixed total data pool. Adding data through participation can improve performance, while fixed-pool repartitioning has task-dependent effects (Figure~\ref{fig:lab-sensitivity}). Proteomics performs best at an intermediate $K$, and Norman degrades as the fixed pool is split across more laboratories. Performance depends on both data access and the partition used for local training.

Both settings use the default design and a fixed training schedule. Increasing participation adds the corresponding local development panels, while fixed-pool repartitioning preserves equal-weight evaluation over all ten original panels. For the cell tasks, local training groups also define the contrastive-negative pool, so partitioning affects the learning objective's available negatives as well as the sequence of updates. Appendix B.1 reports the two settings separately with their corresponding evaluation panels.

\subsection*{4.8 Validation under extended search}
Re-evaluating 14 selected VCC/Tahoe checkpoints reproduces their development scores and the archived test results. Equal-laboratory and pooled-intervention macro scores coincide because every panel contains the same number of interventions. Yet the small development gains transfer poorly: Qwen/VCC gains 1.17 development points but loses 11.89 test points; Tahoe gains 5.00 and loses 11.94. Under 20 new paired development-option rosters, these advantages become $-1.34$ and $-1.33$ points on average. This identifies sensitivity to candidate distractors and option order, rather than an aggregation error. Appendix E reports the conditional variability and distinguishes it from generalization to new biological interventions.
