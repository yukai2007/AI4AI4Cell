# Response to Reviewer cR5g

Thank you for your review and for highlighting the importance of distributed training when laboratories cannot share data. We have reframed the paper around its central contribution, **collaborative model development**: laboratories whose data cannot be pooled jointly configure, train, evaluate and allocate training for one biological predictor using laboratory-local evidence. Section 4.3 now leads with a same-workflow comparison in which the identical BioCoLoop workflow is run by one laboratory alone or by ten laboratories together on the same held-out test, and collaboration improves all five endpoints. Random, Bayesian and exhaustive search are reported both for a single laboratory (Appendix D) and at matched ten-laboratory access (Section 4.7, Appendix E.1-E.2). Section 4.2 now clarifies that "direct" is feedback-free configuration search, not a single fit, and that every method returns one predictor.

## 1. "No comparison to traditional hyperparameter tuning, not even to random search"

**Can one laboratory tune its way to the collaborative result?** No. Appendix D runs random search, GP-EI Bayesian optimization and exhaustive enumeration of the configuration menu on laboratory 0. All remain below collaboration on every endpoint; for example, DTI AUROC is 90.10 for the conventional controls versus **93.76** for collaboration, and proteomics AP is 20.13 for random search versus **36.99**. Holding the history-enabled BioCoLoop workflow fixed, ten laboratories improve on laboratory 0 alone from 89.89 to 93.76 (DTI), 20.13 to 36.99 (proteomics), 20.22 to 31.54 (VCC), 14.15 to 22.25 (Norman) and 15.42 to 22.39 (Tahoe) (Appendix D, Table D.1).

**With the same ten laboratories, how do proposal policies compare?** Appendix E.1-E.2 compares random search without replacement (the exact expectation over all 462 six-design subsets), GP-EI, and direct and history-guided LLM search with identical laboratories, development panels, configuration library and proposal budget; failed LLM requests remain charged. Here the effect of history is task-dependent and conventional search is competitive, which we report plainly. Positive cases include Norman, where Luna history reaches **23.11** Top-1 versus 22.30 for direct and 22.66 for random search, and the gene-disjoint cell benchmark (Appendix E.6), where structured history reaches 22.888 versus 22.625 for direct and 22.668 for random search. On DTI, GP-EI reaches 94.60 and random search 94.45, against 94.27 for history. These controls characterise how proposal policies use shared evidence; the main contribution is collaborative development itself.

## 2. "The laboratories are not real independent entities with a domain shift and batch effects, but random splits of a single dataset"

First, the main-setting laboratories are **not random splits of measurements**. They are grouped by biological unit: DTI laboratories are defined by canonical compounds, proteomic laboratories by compound groups, and cell laboratories hold disjoint sets of training interventions (one mean profile per intervention), so no compound or intervention is shared between laboratories. Development and test interventions are disjoint from training. Simulating clients by partitioning one dataset is the standard protocol in federated-learning research (McMahan et al., 2017; Caldas et al., 2018, LEAF; He et al., 2020, FedML; Beutel et al., 2020, Flower), and Section 4.2 now states this with citations.

Second, we do test the harder case you describe. In **source-defined** experiments, each laboratory is a different released study with its own experimental context, measurement protocol and response objective (Appendix B.2). With one proteomic study per laboratory (PTPC, Lin, Ruprecht and decryptE, combined through a shared representation with source-specific heads), collaboration raises target AP from 34.41 to **37.53** and AUROC from 72.33 to **74.39**. For DTI, adding Davis or Human raises target AUROC from 85.47 to 87.86 and 87.95. For cells, adding the Jiang study raises VCC Top-1 from 29.49 to 33.07. We report every source, including those that do not help, because compatibility between studies matters. Execution is simulated on shared compute, and we do not claim formal privacy guarantees.

## 3. "The authors' own pipeline, when training a single model ('direct'), performs worse than a fixed model"

"Direct" is not a single model fit. It is configuration search without feedback from earlier evaluations: several candidate configurations are trained, and one is retained according to development score and loss. "Fixed" is one predefined configuration. Both return a single predictor. Section 4.2 now states this explicitly.

In the single-laboratory setting, direct search selects using only laboratory 0's development data, which is small. A configuration that wins on that set need not win on the test; for example, single-laboratory direct proteomics scores 20.13 AP versus 25.05 for the fixed model. Collaboration addresses exactly this limitation: with ten laboratories' training and development evidence, BioCoLoop reaches **36.99** AP on the same test. Collaboration also exceeds the single-laboratory fixed model on DTI, proteomics, VCC and Tahoe; on Norman, the fixed configuration (25.56) remains a strong reference, and we report this. Appendix E.3 further audits development selection in the ten-laboratory extended searches and shows that small development gains there can depend on the candidate roster.

## 4. "Other baselines also use 1 model, while BioCoLoop uses 10 labs"

The ten laboratories are data holders, not an ensemble: BioCoLoop, like every baseline, returns **one predictor**, evaluated on the same held-out test. The comparison asks what a laboratory gains by developing that predictor with collaborators instead of alone. This is the standard evaluation in federated learning (Sheller et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2018; Dayan et al., 2021; Ogier du Terrail et al., 2022, FLamby; Warnat-Herresthal et al., 2021; Saldanha et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024, scFed), including federated tuning (Holly et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023, FedHPO-Bench). Section 4.3 now leads with the same BioCoLoop workflow run by one versus ten laboratories (Section 1 above), so that only participation changes.

## 5. "The main approach appears to be asking an LLM to turn 4 knobs"

The contribution is not a new optimizer over four hyperparameters; it is **making model development itself collaborative** across laboratories that cannot pool data. BioCoLoop provides a common interface through which laboratories train candidate models locally, evaluate them on their own development data, and share model updates and bounded development evidence. That evidence then drives configuration selection, subsequent proposals and the allocation of further training, across three biological task families. The original four-factor library is a deliberately controlled instantiation that makes the participation, history and allocation comparisons interpretable. Appendix E.9 widens it to 36 recipes that also vary the prediction head, the training objective and the learning-rate schedule. There, direct search reaches 39.017 proteomics AP versus 37.109 for the fitted default, and DTI search reaches 94.368 AUROC versus 94.271.

Allocation is a second collaborative development decision. At a fixed 800 aggregation rounds and 160 development evaluations, evidence-guided allocation raises Norman Top-1 from 11.48 to **22.32** (+10.84, 95% CI [4.54, 17.73]); simpler promotion rules recover much of this gain over uniform allocation (Appendix E.5). The revised related work distinguishes this laboratory-level collaboration from agent-team systems and from Helmsman's automated synthesis of FL systems, which we acknowledge as directly related prior work.

Thank you again; these comments have clarified both the contribution and the evaluation protocol.
