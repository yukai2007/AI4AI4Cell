# Response to Reviewer AfJB

Thank you for your expert review, for pointing us to closely related work, and for your positive note on the matched-budget allocation experiment. We have reframed the paper around its central contribution, **collaborative model development**: laboratories whose data cannot be pooled jointly configure, train, evaluate and allocate training for one biological predictor using laboratory-local evidence. Section 4.3 now leads with a same-workflow comparison in which the identical BioCoLoop workflow is run by one laboratory alone or by ten laboratories together on the same held-out test, and collaboration improves all five endpoints. Related work adds a paragraph that distinguishes collaboration among data-holding laboratories from agent-team collaboration (The Virtual Biotech, AutoScientists) and acknowledges Helmsman as directly related prior work on automated federated-learning system synthesis. Same-access search controls (Section 4.7) are now presented as a characterisation of how proposal policies use the shared evidence.

## 1. "It is difficult to discern what methodological novelty BioCoLoop introduces" relative to The Virtual Biotech, AutoScientists and Helmsman

All three are discussed in the revised related work. The key distinction is **who collaborates and what they contribute**.

In The Virtual Biotech (Zhang et al., 2026), the collaborators are specialist AI agents that retrieve and integrate biomedical evidence for therapeutic discovery and development decisions. In AutoScientists (Gao, Fang and Zitnik, 2026), self-organizing agent teams share a champion program, an experiment log and failed directions to improve computational models. Both are important precedents for coordinated scientific agents and accumulated experimental history. In both, however, the collaborators are agents working on data they can access; neither addresses the setting in which the training and development data are held by different institutions and cannot be pooled.

In BioCoLoop the collaborators are **data-holding laboratories**. Each laboratory trains candidate configurations on its own data and evaluates them on its own development panels; only model updates and bounded development evidence leave the laboratory. That evidence then determines which configuration is retained, what is proposed next and which candidates receive further training. Model development itself, not only parameter fitting, thus becomes collaborative and data-local.

Helmsman (Li, Funk and Saeed, ICLR 2026) is directly related prior work, and we now say so explicitly. Its collaborating LLM agents plan, generate, and diagnose and revise federated-learning systems through sandboxed federated simulations. BioCoLoop is complementary: rather than synthesizing FL system code, it develops a biological predictor inside a fixed collaborative protocol and uses laboratory-local training and evaluation evidence to configure, select and allocate training for that predictor. The revised related work also cites federated configuration search that predates LLM agents, FedEx (Khodak et al., 2021) and FedNAS (He et al., 2020). We do not claim to be the first agentic or automated FL framework. Our contribution is the collaborative biological model-development setting and interface, together with controlled experiments that separate laboratory participation, proposal history and training allocation.

## 2. "When the number of laboratories is held at one, BioCoLoop performs essentially identically to direct optimization" (Tables A.1-A.3)

Your reading of Table A.1 is correct, and we now explain why this is the expected result. BioCoLoop's mechanism is the use of cross-laboratory development evidence. **A laboratory working alone has no cross-laboratory evidence**, so its proposals and selections largely coincide with feedback-free direct search: 89.89 versus 89.89 on DTI, 20.13 versus 20.13 on proteomics, and 21.09 for direct versus 20.22 on VCC. Appendix A now contains an "Interpreting the single-laboratory control" paragraph that states this.

The informative comparison is therefore the same workflow with and without collaborators. Holding the Qwen history-enabled workflow fixed and changing only participation from laboratory 0 to ten laboratories (Appendix D, Table D.1), DTI AUROC rises from 89.89 to **93.76**, proteomics AP from 20.13 to **36.99**, and VCC, Norman and Tahoe Top-1 from 20.22, 14.15 and 15.42 to **31.54, 22.25 and 22.39**. Tables A.2 and A.3 show the same pattern: DTI AP 87.77 to 92.73, proteomics AUROC 61.80 to 73.87, unseen-drug DTI AUROC 88.39 to 91.65 and unseen-protein DTI AUROC 83.26 to 87.63. More extensive local search does not close this gap: single-laboratory random, Bayesian and exhaustive-menu search remain below collaboration on every endpoint (for example, DTI 90.10 versus 93.76).

## 3. "The main comparison in Table 1 provides BioCoLoop access to ten laboratories, while the existing AI-scientist baselines operate on a single laboratory"

We now state explicitly that this is the intended comparison: what a laboratory gains by developing a model with collaborators rather than alone. Comparing collaborative and single-site training on a common held-out test is the standard evaluation protocol in federated learning (Sheller et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2018; Dayan et al., 2021, EXAM; Ogier du Terrail et al., 2022, FLamby; Warnat-Herresthal et al., 2021; Saldanha et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024, scFed), including federated hyperparameter tuning (Holly et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023, FedHPO-Bench). Section 4.2 now adds this protocol paragraph and clarifies that every method returns one predictor and that "direct" is feedback-free search, not a single fit.

We agree that Table 1 by itself changes both the workflow and the data access. This is why Section 4.3 now leads with the same-workflow comparison above, in which only participation changes. The AI-scientist baselines remain as references for what current agents achieve with one laboratory's data.

## 4. "Across ten comparisons, eight show identical final performance" (Section 4.7)

This observation is accurate, and the revised Section 4.7 reports it plainly. The same-access controls hold the ten laboratories, development panels, configuration library and proposal budget fixed, and they compare history-guided proposals with direct LLM search, random search without replacement and GP-EI (Appendix E.1-E.2). The effect of history is task-dependent and conventional search is competitive. The positive cases are Norman, where Luna history reaches **23.11** Top-1 versus 22.30 for direct and 22.66 for random search; the gene-disjoint cell benchmark (Appendix E.6), where structured history reaches 22.888 versus 22.625 for direct and 22.668 for random search; and the wider-recipe DTI study (Appendix E.9), where Luna structured history reaches 94.368 AUROC versus 94.316 for random search (+0.053 points, conditional 95% interval [0.031, 0.077]). Elsewhere, direct search or GP-EI is equal or better.

We therefore present these controls as characterising how proposal policies use shared evidence, not as the source of the main gain. Section 4.7 now states this separation directly.

## 5. Allocation within collaborative development

The allocation experiment shows a second development decision that collaborative evidence supports. At 800 aggregation rounds and 160 development evaluations for each policy, evidence-guided allocation improves Norman Top-1 from 11.48 to **22.32** (+10.84, 95% CI [4.54, 17.73]). New promotion controls (Appendix E.5) show that simpler promotion rules recover much of this gain over uniform allocation. We present the result as evidence that early laboratory-local measurements are useful for allocating a fixed training budget, not as a new allocation algorithm.
