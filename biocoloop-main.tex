\begin{abstract}
Biological data are often distributed across laboratories that cannot pool their measurements, yet model development---choosing which configurations to train, evaluating them and deciding where to invest further training---is usually performed by a single party with centralized data. We introduce BioCoLoop, a framework that makes model development itself collaborative: laboratories fit and evaluate candidate configurations locally, and the resulting development evidence guides subsequent proposals and training allocation without pooling raw data. Task adapters support drug--target interactions, observed-response proteomic efficacy and cell perturbation identification. Following common federated-learning practice, we compare collaborative development with a laboratory working alone on common held-out tests. The same BioCoLoop workflow improves all five primary endpoints in mean when ten laboratories collaborate, raising DTI AUROC from 89.89 to 93.76 and proteomic AP from 20.13 to 36.99, and single-laboratory random, Bayesian and exhaustive search remain below the collaborative result. Training a fixed default design on all ten laboratories accounts for much of this gain; collaborative design search adds 1.96 AUROC on DTI and 6.77 Top-1 points on double-gene perturbation, where the default design degrades as laboratories are added. When laboratories hold distinct proteomic studies, collaboration raises target AUROC in seven of eight seeds (72.06 to 74.13), with seed-dependent AP gains; gains across distinct DTI and single-cell studies depend on source compatibility. Screening candidates on early collaborative evidence and training the promising ones longer improves double-gene perturbation identification by 10.84 percentage points over uniform allocation at matched training and evaluation budgets. In matched-access controls, language-model-guided search exceeds the exact random-search expectation in mean in all three task families, by task-dependent margins. These results establish a practical framework through which laboratories develop stronger biological predictors together.
\end{abstract}
